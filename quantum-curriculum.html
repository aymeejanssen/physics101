Topic Lessons

Each module gives you the lesson content: what to learn, why it matters, formulas, problem skills, resources, and a checkpoint.

Phase 0: Algebra, Functions, Graphs, and Trigonometry 2-4 weeks

What to learn

  • Solve linear, quadratic, exponential, and logarithmic equations.
  • Use functions: domain, range, inverse, composition, transformations.
  • Use radians, unit circle, sine, cosine, tangent, and core trig identities.
  • Read and sketch graphs without treating them like decoration.

Why it matters for quantum physics

Quantum mechanics uses exponentials, sinusoidal waves, boundary conditions, graphs of probability densities, and symbolic manipulation constantly.

Main formulas

\( \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1 \)
\( e^{a+b}=e^ae^b,\quad \log(ab)=\log a+\log b \)

Checkpoint

  • Complete Khan Academy Algebra 2 and Trigonometry practice.
  • Be able to transform \(y=A\sin(kx+\phi)\) and explain amplitude, frequency, and phase.
Phase 1A: Calculus I, II, and III Core math

What to learn

  • Limits, continuity, derivatives, chain rule, optimization, related rates.
  • Definite and indefinite integrals, substitution, integration by parts, improper integrals.
  • Series, Taylor expansions, partial derivatives, gradients, multiple integrals.
  • Vector calculus basics: divergence, curl, line integrals, surface integrals.

Why it matters

Derivatives describe change; integrals describe totals and probability; multivariable calculus gives you fields, potentials, and wavefunctions in space.

Main formulas

\( \frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1} \)
\( \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a) \)
\( \nabla f=(\partial_x f,\partial_y f,\partial_z f) \)

Problem skills

  • Differentiate and integrate by hand without checking every line.
  • Set up integrals from geometry or physical meaning.
  • Use spherical/cylindrical coordinates when symmetry demands it.
Phase 1B: Linear Algebra Quantum language

What to learn

  • Vectors, matrices, matrix multiplication, Gaussian elimination.
  • Span, basis, dimension, linear independence, vector spaces.
  • Orthogonality, projections, inner products, complex vector spaces.
  • Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, diagonalization, Hermitian and unitary matrices.

Why it matters

Quantum states are vectors. Observables are operators. Measurement outcomes are tied to eigenvalues. This subject is the grammar of quantum theory.

Main formulas

\( A\vec v=\lambda\vec v \)
\( \langle u,v\rangle=u^\dagger v \)
\( A^\dagger=A \quad \text{for Hermitian operators} \)

Checkpoint

Pass MIT 18.06 exams on eigenvalues/eigenvectors and explain why diagonalization simplifies a physical problem.

Phase 1C: Differential Equations, Probability, and Complex Numbers Physics engine

What to learn

  • First-order separable and linear ODEs.
  • Second-order constant-coefficient ODEs and harmonic oscillators.
  • Fourier series basics and separation of variables.
  • Probability distributions, expectation values, variance, complex exponentials.

Why it matters

Schrödinger equations are differential equations. Wavefunctions are complex-valued. Quantum predictions are probability distributions.

Main formulas

\( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\omega^2x=0 \)
\( e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta \)
\( E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x) \)

Checkpoint

Solve oscillator equations, use Euler's formula, and compute expectation values before starting serious QM.

Phase 2: Mechanics, E&M, Waves, and Thermodynamics Classical physics

What to learn

  • Kinematics, Newton's laws, free-body diagrams, energy, momentum, rotation.
  • Electric fields, magnetic fields, potentials, circuits, Maxwell's equations conceptually.
  • Simple harmonic motion, driven/damped oscillators, wave equation, interference.
  • Temperature, heat, entropy, Boltzmann distribution intuition.

Why it matters

Quantum mechanics was built by fixing failures in classical mechanics, waves, and electromagnetism. You need to know what it is replacing.

Main formulas

\( \vec F=m\vec a,\quad K=\frac12mv^2,\quad \vec L=\vec r\times\vec p \)
\( \vec F=q(\vec E+\vec v\times\vec B) \)
\( \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2}=v^2\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} \)

Checkpoint

Work through MIT 8.01, 8.02, and 8.03 exam problems. You should be deriving, not hunting formulas.

Phase 3 and 4: Math Physics and Modern Physics Bridge to QM

What to learn

  • Dirac notation, bras, kets, inner products, operators, commutators.
  • Fourier transforms, delta functions, Hermitian operators, eigenfunction expansions.
  • Special relativity, Lorentz transformations, relativistic energy and momentum.
  • Blackbody radiation, photoelectric effect, de Broglie wavelength, Bohr model.

Why it matters

This layer gives you the language and historical pressure that make quantum mechanics feel necessary instead of arbitrary.

Main formulas

\( [A,B]=AB-BA \)
\( \langle \psi|\phi\rangle=\int \psi^*(x)\phi(x)\,dx \)
\( E^2=(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2,\quad \lambda=\frac{h}{p} \)

Checkpoint

Explain what an operator is, what a commutator means, and why matter waves forced a new theory.

Phase 5: Quantum Mechanics I and II Main target

What to learn

  • Wavefunction interpretation, normalization, expectation values.
  • Time-dependent and time-independent Schrödinger equations.
  • Infinite wells, finite wells, tunneling, harmonic oscillator.
  • Formalism: Hilbert spaces, observables, commutators, uncertainty.
  • Angular momentum, spin, hydrogen atom, approximation methods, scattering.

Why it matters

This is the core of university quantum physics. It turns physical questions into eigenvalue problems and probability amplitudes.

Main formulas

\( i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi=\hat H\Psi \)
\( \hat H\psi=E\psi \)
\( \Delta x\,\Delta p\ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \)

Checkpoint

Derive particle-in-a-box states, compute expectation values, solve standard wells, and take MIT 8.04 exams.

Phase 6: Specializations After Quantum I Optional direction

Quantum Computing

  • Qubits, Bloch sphere, gates, tensor products, entanglement.
  • Use IBM Quantum Learning and Nielsen & Chuang Ch. 1-4.

Quantum Field Theory

  • Relativistic quantum mechanics, fields, creation/annihilation operators, Feynman diagrams.
  • Use Lancaster & Blundell before Peskin & Schroeder.

Condensed Matter

  • Crystal lattices, reciprocal space, phonons, electrons in solids, superconductivity.
  • Use Kittel first, Ashcroft & Mermin later.

Computational Physics

  • Python, NumPy, SciPy, numerical integration, ODEs, eigenvalue simulations.
  • Build particle-in-a-box, tunneling, oscillator, and spin mini-projects.

Chapter Roadmap

This compresses the most important chapter-level lessons. The Study Library popups add the book-specific details.

Foundation and Calculus

First language layer

Functions, graphs, and trigonometry

Learn how formulas behave visually and how waves are described.

  • Master functions, inverse functions, exponentials, logarithms, radians, and identities.
  • Be able to sketch and transform \( \sin x \), \( e^x \), \( \log x \), and polynomial graphs.

Differential and integral calculus

Build the language of change, accumulation, work, area, and probability.

  • Use derivatives for rates, slopes, extrema, velocity, and acceleration.
  • Use integrals for totals, normalization, expectation values, and fields.

Multivariable and vector calculus

Prepare for fields and spatial wavefunctions.

  • Use partial derivatives, gradients, divergence, curl, and coordinate changes.
  • Set up multiple integrals in coordinates that match symmetry.

Series and approximations

Physics often works by approximating intelligently.

  • Learn Taylor series, small-angle approximations, and convergence intuition.
  • Practice estimating before calculating.

Linear Algebra, DEs, and Mathematical Physics

Quantum grammar

Vector spaces and bases

Understand states as objects that can be decomposed into basis components.

  • Use span, basis, independence, orthogonality, and projections.
  • Change basis to make hard problems easier.

Eigenvalues and operators

This is the mathematical heart of measurement in quantum mechanics.

  • Solve eigenvalue problems by hand and numerically.
  • Connect eigenvalues to possible measured quantities.

Oscillators, Fourier, and PDEs

Wave mechanics depends on modes and boundary conditions.

  • Solve oscillator equations and separate variables in simple PDEs.
  • Represent functions as sums of waves.

Dirac notation and Hermitian structure

Move from matrix calculations into the abstract language physicists use.

  • Use bras, kets, inner products, adjoints, commutators, and expectation values.
  • Know why Hermitian operators give real measurement results.

Classical, Modern, and Quantum Physics

Main physics layer

Mechanics and fields

Learn the older framework that quantum theory modifies.

  • Use forces, energy, momentum, angular momentum, potentials, fields, and waves.
  • Understand why Lagrangians and Hamiltonians become powerful later.

Modern physics failures

Study the experimental cracks: blackbody radiation, photoelectric effect, spectra, and matter waves.

  • Know the physical problem each idea solved.
  • Connect \(E=hf\) and \( \lambda=h/p \) to wave-particle duality.

Quantum I

Master wavefunctions, Schrödinger equation, bound states, tunneling, oscillator, and spin basics.

  • Normalize, calculate probabilities, find energies, and interpret measurements.
  • Solve the infinite square well until it feels like a template.

Quantum II

Move into hydrogen, angular momentum addition, identical particles, perturbation, and scattering.

  • Use approximation methods and symmetry to solve realistic systems.
  • Start reading advanced physics as model-building.

Formula Map

Do not memorize these as isolated symbols. Know the story, assumptions, units, and one problem where each is used.

Derivative power rule
\( \frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1} \)

Rates of change, motion, fields, and differential equations.

Definite integral
\( \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \)

Area, work, charge, probability, expectation values, normalization.

Eigenvalue equation
\( A\vec{v}=\lambda\vec{v} \)

The skeleton of quantum states and measured quantities.

Classical oscillator
\( \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}+\omega^2x=0 \)

Bridge from waves and springs to the quantum harmonic oscillator.

Relativistic energy
\( E^2=(pc)^2+(mc^2)^2 \)

Connects energy, momentum, mass, and modern physics.

Schrödinger equation
\( i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi = \hat{H}\Psi \)

The central evolution law of non-relativistic quantum mechanics.

Particle in a box
\( \psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \)

Boundary conditions, normalization, and quantized energy.

Uncertainty principle
\( \Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \)

A structural statement about states, not a bad-instrument problem.

Commutator
\( [A,B]=AB-BA \)

If operators do not commute, order matters and simultaneous certainty can fail.

Custom Written Tests

Use these as written checkpoints. Try without notes first, then open the answer key and repair mistakes.

Math Readiness Test

  1. Differentiate \(f(x)=x^3\sin x\).

  2. Compute \(\int x e^{x^2}\,dx\).

  3. Find the eigenvalues of \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\).

  4. Solve \(d^2x/dt^2+4x=0\).

  5. Evaluate \(e^{i\pi}+1\).

Answers
  • \(3x^2\sin x+x^3\cos x\)
  • \(\frac12 e^{x^2}+C\)
  • \(\lambda=2\) and \(\lambda=5\)
  • \(x(t)=A\cos(2t)+B\sin(2t)\)
  • 0

Classical Physics Test

  1. A 2 kg mass accelerates at \(3\,\text{m/s}^2\). What force acts on it?

  2. Explain conservation of energy for a mass sliding down a frictionless ramp.

  3. Derive the period relationship for a simple harmonic oscillator from \(d^2x/dt^2+\omega^2x=0\).

  4. State what electric potential means in relation to electric field.

Answers
  • \(F=ma=6\,\text{N}\)
  • Potential energy converts to kinetic energy; total mechanical energy stays constant without friction.
  • The solution is sinusoidal with angular frequency \(\omega\), so \(T=2\pi/\omega\).
  • The electric field is related to potential by \(\vec E=-\nabla V\).

Quantum I Test

  1. What does \(|\psi(x)|^2\) represent?

  2. Normalize \(\psi(x)=A\sin(\pi x/L)\) on \(0<x<L\).

  3. What is the physical meaning of an eigenvalue of an observable operator?

  4. State the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

  5. Explain why the infinite square well has quantized energy levels.

Answers
  • Probability density for finding the particle at position \(x\).
  • \(A=\sqrt{2/L}\)
  • A possible measured value of that observable.
  • \(i\hbar\partial\Psi/\partial t=\hat H\Psi\)
  • Boundary conditions allow only standing waves with discrete wavelengths, producing discrete energies.

Weekly Routine

Physics is learned through problem solving. Lectures are only useful when your pencil is moving.

Mon

Math lecture plus 10-15 problems. Rewrite every missed step.

Tue

Physics reading plus 8-12 problems. State assumptions before calculating.

Wed

Math problem set plus formula notes and one derivation from memory.

Thu

Physics lecture plus derivation practice and diagrams.

Fri

Programming or numerical exercise: plot, simulate, diagonalize, compare.

Sat

Long problem-solving block or timed exam section.

Sun

Review errors, make flashcards, rebuild weak topics.

Rule

Never count a topic complete until you solved problems and checked your reasoning.
